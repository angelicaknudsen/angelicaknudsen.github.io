CWD Geothermal Heat Exchanger System

Timeline: Sep 2025-Sep 2026

Location: MIT D-Lab

Supervisor: Susan Murcott

Lab/Group: Geo@MIT

Objective

This project will investigate the impacts of using heat exchangers connected to municipal water systems for thermal energy exchange, focusing on potential changes in water quality during operation. In partnership with the Cambridge Water Department, we will design and construct a pilot-scale heat exchanger system coupled with a compact heater to simulate real-world conditions. The setup will circulate treated tap water through the exchanger, mimicking a closed-loop system representative of municipal configurations. Water samples will be analyzed before and after the heat exchanger to assess any alterations in physical, chemical, and biological properties. This work will provide empirical data to inform future large-scale geothermal or wastewater heat recovery integrations within municipal water infrastructure.

Outcomes and Contributions

Pilot-scale, Mobile Version

The most recent iteration involves a pilot-scale, mobile experiment.

For cost-effectiveness, we custom-built the cart using 2x4 wooden planks for the frame, plywood for the lower level floor, and 1x3s for supporting the expansion tank and the PEX tubing. I modeled this cart in Solidworks, and designed it to withstand 200+ lbs. of force, as the heat exchanger and heater weigh a combined 100+ lbs.

Figure 1: Completed CAD of the cart

The next phase of the project involved designing the revamped system, now constrained to fit on this cart. I was the lead on modeling this system in Solidworks. The system contains hundreds of components, necessitating computationally-friendly CAD assembly techniques. Below is the most recent iteration of the system model.

Figure 2: Completed CAD of both ambient (red) and CWD (blue) loops

With the CAD, we began building the water line analog to MIT's ambient water loop system, which contains a heater, expansion tank, a flow meter, pump, and thermocouple holders. To control the water temperature, we will implement a PID controller for the heater. The water quality on this side is not as crucial to our experiment, which is why temperature and flow rate are the only two qualities tracked here.

During the entire course of the summer, I built the system with the help of my graduate student mentor (we both had other full-time commitments, which is why the progress was relatively slow). By the end of the summer, we finished the loop.

Figure 3: Completed ambient water loop

Since this picture, we added a heater drip pan. The open T's are where the thermocouples will be held, with a work-in-progress probe holder.

Bench-top, Stationary Version

I thought to add this here since this CAD took a substantial amount of time.

Initially, we anticipated that we had enough lab space for a stationary, bench-top scale experiment. Below are pictures and an interactive of this version of the CAD, which contains an open water line for the Cambridge municipal water side, and a closed-loop water line representing the MIT ambient water loop.

We realized that to reduce water waste and increase flexibility, we needed to make the experiment fully closed-loop and more vertical on a mobile cart. This is so that refilling the water lines and moving the experiment where space allows could be more feasible.

Also, the heat exchanger we selected turned out to be the same size as one that would be implemented for an actual pilot. From this, we decided to lean into sizing components to be pilot-scale. This means that this project's experimental results will be more representative of the actual proposed system's implementation and therefore potentially more effective in promoting the proposed thermal energy network at MIT and beyond.

Figure 4: CAD of initial significant iteration
